Something up there is moving.

NASA's SPHEREx telescope photographs the whole sky in 102 infrared colours every six months. This tool searches its images for things that move, from fast main-belt asteroids to slow, distant Planet-X-class worlds, and grades every result against JPL's catalogue.

02 Planet X mode

Slow, distant, and still found.

A Planet X at hundreds of au barely moves: well under three arcseconds an hour. We search a whole week of visits for those crawlers, using a different trick: an uncatalogued source that is missing from the median of the other visits at the same spot. We test it on the real thing, a dwarf planet 50 au away, and plant synthetic worlds in the same real images, drawn with SPHEREx's own PSF, to measure how faint a Planet X could be.

    04 Only SPHEREx can do this

    A spectrum for every rock.

    SPHEREx has no filter wheel: each detector row sees a different wavelength, and every visit nudges the sky across those rows. A passing asteroid leaves 15 to 40 spectral points from 0.75 to 5 µm. We fit reflected sunlight with a slope, plus heat at the temperature its real distance from the Sun implies, and compare the slope with JPL's published class and albedo.

    07 Method

    Three ideas, no magic.

    i.

    Read only what we need

    Each SPHEREx image is 69 MB. We read headers, then fetch just the pixel rows around our targets with HTTP range requests: kilobytes per asteroid, straight from NASA's IRSA archive. Section 05 does the same thing live, in front of you.

    ii.

    Two searches, two speeds

    Fast movers trip SPHEREx's own outlier flags; we seed on them and link straight-line motion across three or more visits in about 2.6 days. Slow movers do not, so we seed on uncatalogued sources missing from the other visits' median and link across a week.

    iii.

    Grade everything

    JPL predicts every known body as seen from SPHEREx's orbit. We report completeness by brightness, scramble detections for the chance rate, reject detector-fixed tracks, and plant synthetic movers with the real PSF to measure depth.

    Q1Why not just subtract one image from another?

    Because SPHEREx sees a different wavelength in every detector row, the same star changes brightness between exposures. Straight subtraction leaves a sky full of false residuals. Outlier flags, multi-visit medians and motion linking avoid that.

    Q2Did you discover a new object?

    Not yet, and we can prove it. Every unidentified track is compared with the number noise alone produces when we scramble our detections, and rejected if it reuses one detector pixel. A real discovery has to beat that, then get follow-up and a Minor Planet Center report.

    Q3How does Planet X mode work, and what can it see?

    Over a week a body at 50 to 500 au moves 8 to 55 pixels, enough to link. Within a pass, not across six months: half a year of Earth's own orbit shifts a distant object by degrees, which needs an orbit search. Section 02 shows the real dwarf-planet test and the measured depth for synthetic Planet-X-like movers.

    Q4Doesn't the PSF grow with wavelength and fake the spectrum?

    We measured it with SPHEREx's own PSF, stored in every file for 121 detector zones: a 3-pixel aperture holds more than 99.6% of the light at every wavelength from 0.9 to 4.7 µm, because SPHEREx's 6.15″ pixels dominate the blur. Spectra now use PSF-weighted fits with that per-zone PSF (about 1.4× the SNR of an aperture) and the local pixel solid angle from the distortion solution (which varies −1% to +1.6% across a detector). Spin is real: points from different visits carry a ±0.15 mag rotation term.

    Q5How do you know the spectra mean anything?

    Our fitted red slope rises with JPL's published albedo: dark, primitive asteroids come out flat; bright S-types come out red. Section 06 reports the exact sample, the selection rule, a permutation p-value and a Monte Carlo interval that re-fits every slope under noise and spin. We never use JPL's classes in the fit.

    Q6What about detector persistence, the dichroic edge, and static red sources?

    Seeds touching SPHEREx's persistence, dichroic-edge, hot or cosmic-ray flags anywhere in their 3×3 core are dropped. Tracks that reuse one detector pixel are rejected. Tracks with any point sitting on a source detected at the same sky spot in another visit are rejected as stationary, which removes very red static sources that only appear at some wavelengths. Slow-mode comparisons skip visits too close in time to show motion.
