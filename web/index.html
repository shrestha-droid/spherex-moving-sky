Something up there is moving.

NASA's SPHEREx telescope photographs the whole sky in 102 infrared colours every six months. We search its images for things that move between exposures, and then grade ourselves against every known asteroid in JPL's catalogue.

02 Found blind, then identified

The tracks ledger.

Every track our detector linked across three or more visits, with no catalogue hints. Afterwards each one is checked against JPL. Matches score the detector; anything unmatched is weighed against the false-alarm rate.

    03 Only SPHEREx can do this

    A spectrum for every rock.

    SPHEREx has no filter wheel: each detector row sees a different wavelength, and each visit nudges the sky across those rows. So a passing asteroid leaves behind a spectrum from 0.75 to 5 µm. Rocky and carbon-rich asteroids reflect sunlight differently, and the longest wavelengths start to catch their heat.

    05 Method

    Three ideas, no magic.

    i.

    Read only what we need

    Each SPHEREx image is 69 MB. We read headers, then fetch just the pixel rows around our targets with HTTP range requests: a few kilobytes per asteroid, straight from NASA's IRSA archive.

    ii.

    Let the pipeline point

    SPHEREx's own processing flags pixels that disagree with overlapping exposures. Moving objects trip that flag. We seed on it, skip catalogued stars, and link seeds that line up in straight-line motion across three or more visits.

    iii.

    Grade everything

    JPL predicts every known asteroid as seen from SPHEREx's orbit. We measure each one, report completeness by brightness, and scramble our own detections to measure the false-alarm rate.

    Q1Why not just subtract one image from another?

    Because SPHEREx sees a different wavelength in every detector row, the same star changes brightness between exposures. Straight subtraction leaves a sky full of false residuals. Outlier flags plus multi-visit linking avoid that.

    Q2Did you discover a new object?

    Not yet, and we can prove it. Any unmatched track is compared with the number of tracks noise alone produces when we scramble the detections. A real discovery has to beat that rate, then get follow-up and a Minor Planet Center report.

    Q3What about Planet X?

    Distant worlds move under two arcseconds per hour, too slowly to separate from drifting stars within a few days. SPHEREx returns to every field each six months; linking those revisits is how a search for slow, distant movers would work, and it is our next step.

    Q4How accurate are the spectra?

    Wavelengths come straight from SPHEREx's per-pixel calibration table. Fluxes use a fixed aperture with no PSF correction yet, so the longest wavelengths read somewhat low, and an asteroid's spin can change its brightness between visits. Shapes are indicative, not publication-grade.
